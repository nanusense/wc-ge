Living with Snakes at Malhar

Malhar shares its ground with more kinds of snake than most residents ever notice, most of them shy and wanting nothing to do with us, all of them older residents of this land than we are. When one turns up in a garden or a stairwell, the first call here isn't to chase it away. It's to a neighbour who comes out, gently, and moves it on.

This page is not a warning. It's a record: of what lives here, what to do if you meet one, and how this community has learned to share the ground with them.

If you see one

What to do if you see a snake

The Malhar protocol. Six steps, the same every time, whether it's a wolf snake on the stairs or a cobra in the garden.

1

Stay calm, keep your distance

Most snakes want to leave undetected, not to fight. Give it room and it usually takes it.

2

Don't corner or provoke it

No poking, no throwing things, no trying to catch or kill it yourself. Cornering is what causes bites.

3

Note the block, not the address

Which block and roughly what it looks like is enough. Exact house numbers aren't necessary.

4

Call the helpline

The Goodearth Snake Helpline (WhatsApp) reaches a trained rescuer directly, day or night.

5

Keep the area lit and clear

Keep pets and children back, and a torch on the spot if it's after dark, until the rescuer arrives.

6

It's identified and released

Safely, away from homes, unharmed.

A resident's primer

Living with snakes

The myth-versus-fact essentials, if you only read one section on this page.

  • Of the species recorded here, close to twenty so far, only three are medically significant. The rest are non-venomous, whatever they look like.
  • The snakes people see most often here are non-venomous. The Rat Snake and the Wolf Snake account for most helpline calls, and neither is venomous.
  • Snakes follow rodents and cover, not people. Keep leaf litter, firewood piles, and gaps under gates and drains clear along your compound wall, and you remove most of what draws them close.

A snake in the garden is not an emergency. It's a neighbour that hasn't learned to knock.— the Goodearth Snake Helpline

The keeper's journey

A record kept since 2022

It didn't start as a calling. By his own account, it began with curiosity around 2020, a first snake handled bare-handed the following year, and by 2023 the hook, tongs, and training of a serious volunteer. What keeps Sandeep going, four years and hundreds of rescues later, is less an appetite for adventure and more a conviction: that every life, and Malhar's biodiversity, is worth the calls that come at 2am. Every rescue since 2022 is written up, one at a time, in his own words.
